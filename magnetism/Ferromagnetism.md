Ferromagnetism comes down to quantum mechanics and the specific electronic structure of transition metals like iron, nickel, and cobalt.

While every electron has an intrinsic magnetic moment (spin) and an orbital angular momentum, in most materials these moments cancel out in pairs or point in random directions, resulting in a net magnetic field of zero.

Iron is different because of two main factors: unpaired electrons and the exchange interaction.

1. The Electron Configuration of Iron

Iron (atomic number 26) has an electron configuration of $[Ar] 3d^6 4s^2$. The action happens in the $3d$ shell, which can hold up to 10 electrons. In an isolated iron atom, the $3d$ orbitals contain four unpaired electrons. According to Hund's rules, electrons in unfilled orbitals prefer to align their spins parallel to each other to minimize electrostatic repulsion and lower their quantum mechanical energy state.

2. The Exchange Interaction and Crystal Lattices

In a solid ferrous material, atoms form a crystal lattice. The outer $4s$ electrons form a conduction band, but the $3d$ orbitals overlap with neighboring atoms.

Direct overlap usually favors antiparallel alignment (antiferromagnetism), but in iron, the interatomic distance relative to the radius of the $3d$ orbital is just right. This creates a positive exchange interaction—a quantum mechanical effect driven by the Coulomb interaction and the Pauli exclusion principle. It energetically favors parallel alignment of the spin magnetic moments of neighboring iron atoms over short distances.

This creates exchange coupling: adjacent atoms force each other's spins into alignment, forming microscopic regions called magnetic domains.

3. Domains and the Net Field

Within a single domain, millions of iron atoms have their $3d$ electron spins pointing in the exact same direction. However, in an unmagnetized piece of iron, these domains point in random directions, canceling each other out.

When you apply an external magnetic field:
- Domain wall motion: Domains that are already aligned with the external field grow larger by absorbing neighboring domains.
- Rotation: The magnetic moments of entire domains rotate to align with the applied field.

Ferrous materials are exceptional at this because their crystal structure and electronic makeup allow these domains to form easily and to "lock" into alignment (retentivity) even after the external field is removed, creating permanent magnets, or to snap back to a randomized state (soft magnetism).

It is the specific geometry of the iron atom's $d$-shell electrons interacting with its neighbors in the lattice that amplifies quantum-scale spin into macroscopic force.
