Case Study: Terrestrial Planet Genesis and Solid-Phase Nodal Mechanics

Application of Scalar Relaxation Cosmology (SRC) to Earth and Generic Rocky Body System Architectures

1. Executive Summary

Standard planetary science models terrestrial planet formation as a stochastic, bottom-up agglomeration sequence: dust grains collide via electrostatic cling, cross the unmodeled "meter-size barrier" through turbulent concentration (streaming instabilities), grow into kilometer-scale planetesimals, and undergo violent, chaotic giant impacts over tens of millions of years. Differentiation into a metallic core, silicate mantle, and crust is treated as a late-stage byproduct of gravitational settling and radioactive decay heating in an assumed molten state.

Scalar Relaxation Cosmology (SRC) provides a unified, top-down alternative. Rocky bodies are solid-phase electromagnetic nodes (slag-nodes) forged directly within high-energy Z-pinch compression regions along the stellar-planetary circuit rails.

Where outer gas giants (e.g., Jupiter) represent high-capacity, diffuse-gradient flux traps that entrain vast volatile envelopes, inner terrestrial bodies represent high-current-density, low-impedance refining nodes. Extreme electromagnetic pinch pressures force immediate phase transitions into dense metallic conductors and dielectric silicates, while volatile species are electrodynamically excluded by high localized potential gradients.

Earth serves as the structural baseline for this universal class of rocky exoplanets.

3.1. The Central Conductive Anvil (The Core)

Under the intense pinch pressure of the circuit node, materials with the highest electrical conductivity ($\sigma$) and optimal mass-to-charge ratios are compressed along the central axis of the pinch:
- Iron, nickel, and highly conductive siderophile alloys are focused at the focal node.
- This is not a slow gravitational "settling" through cold rock; it is instantaneous, field-driven electromagnetic separation.
- The core functions as the conductive anchor of the planetary node, providing the high-conductivity path necessary to couple with the interplanetary magnetic circuit.

3.2. The Dielectric Silicate Mantle (The Insulator)

Surrounding the conductive core, lower-conductivity refractory species (silicon, oxygen, magnesium, aluminum) form an insulating dielectric shell:
- The core-mantle boundary (CMB) represents a fundamental impedance discontinuity.
- Rather than being a purely thermal transition zone, the CMB serves as the interface between the high-conductivity internal circuit element and the capacitive dielectric envelope.
- Crustal formation represents the outer brittle, crystalline boundary condition where the scalar relaxation of the pinch reached its low-pressure equilibrium.

3.3. Volatile Depletion and the Potential Gradient

Mainstream models rely on external stellar "photo-evaporation" or distant "frost lines" to explain why terrestrial bodies lack massive hydrogen-helium atmospheres.

In SRC, volatile depletion is an inherent electrodynamic property of inner nodes:
- The high current density and steep potential gradient near the stellar anode generate strong Lorentz forces that drive light, easily ionized volatiles ($H, He$) outward along the circuit rails.
- Volatiles are swept to outer, high-capacity nodes (the gas giants) where the potential gradient is broader and local impedance supports extended diffuse envelopes.
- Terrestrial bodies retain only heavy volatiles ($N_2, CO_2, H_2O$) trapped within the dielectric mantle matrix during rapid crystal phase locking.

4. Universal Scaling for Exoplanetary Systems (Kepler Context)

By framing Earth as a specific instance of a generic slag-node, the wide diversity of terrestrial and rocky exoplanets mapped in the kiss-exoplanet-visualizer becomes a predictable continuum rather than an array of accidental collisions:

1. Iron-Enriched Inner Nodes (Mercury Analogs): Nodes forming closest to the stellar anode experience maximum current density and higher Z-pinch compression. The fraction of central conductive slag (iron core) relative to dielectric mantle increases systematically with circuit proximity.
2. Super-Earths in Tight Orbits: Kepler observed numerous multi-planet systems containing massive rocky worlds parked at orbits within 0.1 AU. Core-accretion models require fine-tuned, multi-stage disk migration to park these worlds without having them plunge into the host star. In SRC, these bodies formed in situ at high-frequency, high-impedance nodal harmonic points of the stellar circuit.
3. The Radius Valley (1.5 to 2.0 $R_\oplus$): The distinct gap between rocky cores and mini-Neptunes observed in Kepler survey data reflects the phase boundary between solid-state slag nodes and diffuse volatile traps. A node either possesses sufficient potential well depth to hold an extended plasma envelope, or it drops below the threshold and solidifies cleanly as a bare dielectric-metallic node.
